# Redraw Burton's synopses as live brace sheets

Burton's synopses should be re-set as type, not shown as pictures or flattened into lists: HTML text boxes laid out left to right, one column per level, joined by drawn braces, with every Sect./Memb./Subs. leaf a link and every continuation sign (♈, ♊, ♋, A, B, C) a link to its continuation table. For the front page, the best fit with the owner's choice of "three compartments, as in the engraving" is three framed compartments that each hold the opening brace of one Partition and unfold into that Partition's full sheet on demand. A vertical brace outline is still needed for phones, print and screen readers. On chapter pages, "where this page stands" works best as a small fragment of the sheet: the chain of braces from the Partition down to the current subsection, inked in rubric, which opens the full sheet at that node. These choices follow from three findings. The brace table is a spatial form whose meaning depends on columns and braces, which an indented outline loses. I found no digital edition that makes such a table navigable while keeping its braces. The HCI studies favour aligned, left-to-right text and stable positions, and they argue against radial, hyperbolic and space-filling layouts for long labels. The edition's own data fits this: 489 nodes across three synopses, up to eight levels deep, with median labels of 36–47 characters and 169 nodes that already point at a division of the book.

## Burton's tables descend from Galenic medical tables

The bracketed *tabula* reads left to right and from general to particular. A general term on the left divides through a brace into two, three or four members, and each member divides again "jusqu'au bout de la page" ([Chevalier, *Ramifier sa pensée*, ENSSIB 2023](https://www.enssib.fr/bibliotheque-numerique/documents/71805-ramifier-sa-pensee-l-edition-des-diagrammes-de-pierre-de-la-ramee.pdf)). Ramus did not invent the form. **Some of the first printed logical diagrams appear in Phrissemius's edition of Agricola (Paris: Wechel, 1529)**, and in one copy of Latomus's brace-less *Epitome* a reader drew the braces in by hand. Physicians used the form before the Ramists did: **Fuchs printed dichotomies in 1538 and Dubois tabulated Galen in 1539**, and Maclean's survey shows medicine as the field where such diagrams were most common (Chevalier, pp. 44–45, same source). Ong treated Ramist division as "class logic in space" ([Ong, *Ramus, Method, and the Decay of Dialogue*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/R/bo3627435.html)). Angelini, following Ong, Yates and Rossi, reads the page of tree diagrams as a "mental topic" that replaced the memory palace ([Angelini, *Galilæana* 2024](https://gal-studies.museogalileo.it/index.php/galilaeana/article/download/45/37)). Gowland places Burton's "visually striking synoptic tables" in this medical line, "of a kind found in other late Renaissance medical works", and ties their vocabulary of Partition, Section, Member and Subsection to the art of anatomy ([Gowland, Handbook chapter, 2017](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10054813/1/Gowland_%5BHandbook%20of%20English%20Renaissance%20Literature%5D%2025.%20Robert%20Burton%2C%20The%20Anatomy%20of%20Melancholy%20%281621%E2%80%931651%29.pdf)).

Burton's tables differ from Zwinger's in a way that matters for design. Zwinger's very long diagrams gave no page references, and Blair calls them a conceptual guide rather than a finding aid. Keckermann found them unusable beside the index (Chevalier, p. 128, citing Blair). **Burton's leaves carry Sect./Memb./Subs. numbers**, so his synopsis is at once a picture of the division and an analytical table of contents. His tables also split into continuation tables. In the First Partition the leaf "Melancholy" ends "See ♈", a second table headed "Melancholy: in which consider" carries on from it, and the leaves for causes, symptoms and prognostics point on to tables A, B and C ([Project Gutenberg #10800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10800); [Ex-Classics, First Partition synopsis](https://www.exclassics.com/anatomy/anat12.htm)). Gowland's introduction describes the reader's use plainly. Linear reading is "neither required nor particularly appropriate", and the tables "perform the role of a modern 'table of contents'" that lets readers "move easily from one area to another" ([Penguin sample, pp. xx–xxi](https://cdn.penguin.co.uk/dam-assets/books/9780141192284/9780141192284-sample.pdf)). Fish reads the subdivisions the other way, as false promises of order that the discourse dissolves ([Wells, *Robert Burton's Rhetoric*, ch. 1](https://www.psupress.org/sample_chapter/Wells_chapter1.pdf)). A design that serves both readings shows the synopsis three ways: as a **map** seen whole, as a **launch point** whose leaves open chapters, and as a **chain** of continuation tables followed by their signs.

The edition's data (`data/synopses/p1.json`, `p2.json`, `p3.json`, counted for this report) sets the scale. The top level of each file is a list of continuation tables, so the signs are already part of the structure.

| Synopsis | Tables | Nodes | Max depth | Leaves | Nodes with a division link | Median / longest label (chars) |

The widest level holds 50 nodes (depth 4 of the First Partition), and seven or eight levels occur in every Partition. Neither the full depth nor the full width will fit on one screen at reading size, which rules out any design that requires the whole tree to be visible at once.

An indented outline keeps the parent–child nesting and loses six things. It loses the whole table seen at once on a closed page, the brace as the visible act of division, alignment of same-rank terms in one column, the continuation signs as jumps, the typographic hierarchy that Zwinger and Savigny used as an extra level, and the "Or" connectives that label a brace. The EEBO-TCP encoders state the trade-off in their own guidelines. Nested lists are "usually the preferred method", while a table "captures the appearance, rather than the logic" and "misrepresents the original" ([TCP list guidelines](https://www.textpartnership.net/docs/dox/lists.html)). An interactive edition does not have to choose between them, because it can draw the appearance from the logic.

## No edition yet makes a brace table navigable with its braces kept

Existing renderings of Burton's synopses fall into four kinds, and none is interactive. Page images exist in the 1621 scans on [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1475-1640_the-anatomy-of-melanchol_burton-robert_1621) and the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.anatomyofmelanch00burt/?sp=70&st=text). Gutenberg's plain text sets each node as a separate line, recoverable only from "Or" and the division labels ([Gutenberg #10800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10800)). **Ex-Classics renders the First Partition synopsis as a `<PRE>` block of asterisk bullets**, with the Aries sign corrupted to "W" ([Ex-Classics](https://www.exclassics.com/anatomy/anat12.htm)). Exhibits such as "Curing Melancholy" show scans with prose commentary and no zoom, hotspots or text links ([Omeka exhibit](https://earlymodernscience.omeka.net/exhibits/show/curingmelancholy/alookinsidetheanatomyofmelanch)). TEI simplePrint can say that text is "marked with a brace on the left side" (`simple:leftbraced`), but it does not model the layout ([TEI simplePrint](https://tei-c.org/release/doc/tei-p5-exemplars/html/tei_simplePrint.doc.html)). The LaTeX `schemata` package typesets scholastic and Ramist brace outlines for print only ([schemata documentation](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/texlive/Contents/live/texmf-dist/doc/generic/schemata/schemata.pdf)). Blair's *Too Much to Know* reproduces Zwinger's branching table with her own glosses in a sans-serif margin, which is the scholarly model of reading a brace table for others ([NYU page reproducing Blair's figure](https://cs.nyu.edu/faculty/davise/DataStructures/Zwinger.html)).

The closest design exemplars come from outside early modern scholarship. **Nicholas Rougeux's Byrne's Euclid redraws all 269 of Byrne's 1847 diagrams as SVG in Caslon**, keeps the long s and Byrne's symbols, and aims to stay "as true as possible to its design" with "just a few conveniences". It also offers a proof menu that uses the miniature diagrams as thumbnails ([Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2018/12/a-beautifully-designed-edition-of-euclids-elements.html); [My Modern Met](https://mymodernmet.com/byrnes-euclid-nicholas-rougeux); [FlowingData](https://flowingdata.com/2018/12/18/modern-reproduction-of-1847-geometry-books)). His Iconographic Encyclopaedia puts a hotspot overlay on each engraved plate, so clicking a figure says what it is and where the text mentions it ([c82.net, about, via search extract](https://www.c82.net/iconography/about)). The ARTFL Encyclopédie pairs its frontispiece with Diderot's "Explication du Frontispice", which names the figures by position ([ARTFL](https://encyclopedie.uchicago.edu/node/170)). This is the nearest structural twin to Burton's frontispiece and his ten-stanza "Argument". The Shelley-Godwin Archive sets page image and transcription side by side ([S-GA](https://shelleygodwinarchive.org/using-the-archive/)). Together these give the edition a working pattern. The engraving stays a photograph with a quiet overlay. The synopses are re-set in the edition's type with real braces, as Rougeux did for Byrne. The source image of each table stays one click away, as a check on the re-setting.

## Aligned left-to-right text beats radial and space-filling layouts

The HCI evidence was gathered on short labels (file names, taxa), and **no study found tests sentence-length labels**. What it does show bears on Burton. In SpaceTree's study (18 users, a tree of more than 7,000 nodes), the horizontal node-link tree beat Explorer at listing a node's ancestors (6.8 s against 11.4 s). On judging which branch was larger, users made 2 errors with SpaceTree, 10 with the hyperbolic browser and 12 with Explorer. The authors credit "the alignment of the labels, allowing faster scanning". Explorer was fastest at returning to a visited node (6.5 s against 15 s for SpaceTree and 22.7 s for hyperbolic), because it kept several branches open and never moved a node. The hyperbolic tree moved nodes between visits, and its labels were "not aligned and sometimes overlap" ([Plaisant, Grosjean & Bederson 2002](https://obj.umiacs.umd.edu/spacetree/2002-05.pdf)). Users rejected continuous geometric zoom bluntly: "make it readable or don't bother showing the nodes". They resented opening one level at a time, and they misread SpaceTree's triangle glyph for collapsed branches. Kobsa's 48-user comparison of six systems found Windows Explorer the fastest (101.2 s per task) and rated well above Treemap on satisfaction. Its users abandoned a 3-D tree for its outline panel, partly because the outline linked to the picture but not back ([Kobsa 2004](https://ics.uci.edu/~kobsa/papers/2004-InfoVis-kobsa.pdf)). Eye-tracking work on ontologies finds indented lists better for search and node-link graphs better for "information processing" ([Fu, Noy & Storey](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/content/eye-tracking-user-experience-evaluation-ontology-visualization-techniques-0)).

Burton's printed table already is a left-to-right node-link tree with aligned labels, so the evidence supports a brace sheet for showing structure and path, and an outline linked to it for lookup and return. Four techniques should be ruled out for the reading surface. Sunburst and treemap make "peripheral files ... tiny and hard to tell apart" ([Stasko et al. 2000](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~john.stasko/papers/ijhcs00.pdf)), and they would rotate or crop Burton's sentences. Hyperbolic and fisheye views move nodes between visits, which costs return trips. Free pan-and-zoom canvases leave non-experts "lost in the fog". The icicle plot, a rectangular partition laid out left to right, is the one space-filling layout that keeps text horizontal and puts each level in its own column. It looks like a brace table drawn with boxes, and it suits a label-free thumbnail or minimap. No study of icicles with long labels was found.

The same studies supply rules for opening and orientation. Expand by clicking a node, and open as many levels as fit. Never reorder siblings, and keep relative positions stable. Animate in stages: SpaceTree trims, translates and then grows, which drew "our most positive feedback". Highlight the ancestor path. Summarise a hidden branch with a count rather than a glyph. DOITrees add a breadcrumb trail of ancestors from which nodes "fall" into place, and highlighting of newly revealed nodes that fades out ([Heer & Card 2004](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/2004-DOITree-AVI.pdf)). Animated transitions helped users reconstruct the space at no cost in time ([Bederson & Boltman 1999](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/984)). The W3C tree pattern gives the keyboard model if the sheet becomes a widget: Right opens, Left closes or goes to the parent, type-ahead, and `*` to open siblings ([WAI-ARIA APG Tree View](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/treeview/)).

The build needs no heavy library, and probably none at all. **d3-hierarchy is ISC-licensed and about 5.6 kB gzipped** ([Bundlephobia](https://bundlephobia.com/package/d3-hierarchy)). d3-flextree handles nodes of different heights in O(n) time, but it is WTFPL-licensed and pins d3-hierarchy v1 ([package.json](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/d3-flextree@2.1.2/package.json)). markmap pulls in the whole of d3 v7 and draws mind-map curves ([markmap-view package.json](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/markmap-view@0.17.2/package.json)), and elkjs is a general graph engine that a strict tree of 200 nodes does not need. My own inference, not from the sources: a brace table is a nested flex layout. Each node is a row holding its label, a brace and a column of its children, and the brace is an SVG path stretched to the height of the children's column. CSS does that layout without JavaScript. The text stays real HTML, so Ctrl-F, selection, hyphenation and `lang` on Latin runs keep working, which the V4 language check requires. JavaScript is then needed only to open and close branches and to animate them. Width remains the constraint. Eight columns at 220–300 px each come to about 1,800–2,400 px (inference), so the sheet must open partially on desktop and hand over to the outline on phones.

## Five front-page designs, from compartment to full sheet

Round 5 chose "three compartments, as in the engraving" (F3 B), and round 6 tried four arrangements of them (`state/review/ab-2026-11-04-r6.html`). The owner then asked for something matching "both Burton's original and our front page". The front page puts the 1628 engraving large on the left, so the compartments carry the engraving's language: ruled frames, a centred title in capitals, the plate's sober ink. What goes inside them carries Burton's: set type, braces, rubric for the signs. The options below can be combined, and the recommendation follows the table.

| Option | What the reader sees | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Compartments that unfold** | Three ruled compartments under or beside the engraving. Each shows its Partition's title and first brace in type (the First Partition's "In diseases consider…" with its members). A click unfolds that compartment into the full sheet beneath, opening as many levels as fit. Continuation signs scroll to their table with the path inked. | Keeps the owner's choice and the engraving's frames. Burton's braces are visible before any click. One open sheet at a time keeps positions stable. | The first brace alone does not show the shape of the whole. The unfold needs careful staged animation and an anchored scroll. |
| **2. Brace columns beside the engraving** | Miller columns drawn as braces in the right-hand column: the three Partitions, then a brace into the chosen one's members, then the next. The oldest column slides off left behind a small brace crumb. | Fits the space beside a tall engraving and keeps reading left to right. Each step is one brace, which is Burton's own move. | Shows three or four levels at a time, never the sheet, and loses the compartment frames the owner chose. |
| **3. The whole sheet, re-set** | A Byrne-style redraw of each synopsis at full width, scrolling sideways, its continuation tables as separate sheets joined by their signs, with the source image a click away. | The most faithful, and the best "map". It has a home already: the per-Partition synopsis page (`/pN/synopsis/`). | Too wide for the front page, and on a phone it needs the outline instead. |
| **4. Brace outline** | Vertical nesting with a drawn brace down the left of each group, small capitals for Sect./Memb., division links as links. | Works at 360 px, prints, and suits screen readers. Explorer-like return trips. | Loses Burton's columns. It is needed as the fallback, but it does not answer the owner's request. |
| **5. Engraving to branch** | Quiet hotspots on the engraving's compartments: hover or focus shows the stanza of the Argument (already built), and a click opens the branch of the synopsis it illustrates. | Joins picture and diagram both ways, as c82 joins plate and text and ARTFL joins image and explication. | The compartment-to-branch mapping is an editorial reading, not attested in the sources gathered, and some compartments (the portrait, the title) have no branch. |

The recommendation is option 1 on the front page, with option 3 as the page it unfolds into and option 4 below about 700 px, in print and for assistive technology. Option 5 is worth adding only where the edition states the mapping and labels it as its own. The sources gathered give no scholarly mapping from compartments to branches, so any link from Inamorato to love-melancholy or Hypochondriacus to the hypochondriacal species has to be recorded in `data/manual/` as an editorial decision, in keeping with the honesty rules. Option 1 meets the owner's two tests. It looks like the front page because it uses the engraving's frames, and it looks like Burton because the frames hold his braces in type, not a summary of them. It also serves the three readings: the closed compartments give the map, the leaves are launch points, and the signs make the chain.

A sixth variant is worth one A/B pair: inside each closed compartment, a label-free icicle silhouette of the Partition, one column per level and one box per node, drawn in hairline rules. It shows the shape and size of each Partition at a glance, which SpaceTree's users judged poorly with outlines. It becomes the sheet when opened. Its risk is that it reads as a modern chart rather than as print. The A/B should set it against option 1's "first brace in type".

Several details apply to every option. Leaves with a division link should look like links. Structural nodes should not. Counts ("+14") should replace triangles on closed branches. Opening a branch should keep the clicked node where it was on screen, and `prefers-reduced-motion` should skip the animation. Siblings must never move. The braces should be drawn as paths that taper to a point at the middle, like the printed braces that compositors assembled from rules and corner pieces ([Piggin, "Curly braces"](https://macrotypography.blogspot.com/2015/11/curly-braces.html)), not as CSS borders with rounded corners.

## "Where this page stands" as a fragment of the sheet

Round 4 chose N1 D, "a mark that opens the branch", at the top left of each chapter page. The research suggests four forms for that mark, and the first two deserve the next A/B. All four share one behaviour: a click opens the Partition's sheet at this node, with its ancestors open and the path inked, by `#id` and `:target`. That gives the round trip that Kobsa's users missed.

The **brace path** draws the chain from the Partition through Sect., Memb. and Subs. to this page as a small run of braces opening rightwards. Each step is one word or a numeral in small capitals, the current step is in rubric, and the step's siblings appear as faint ticks behind each brace with a count. It is DOITree's breadcrumb drawn in Burton's own form, and it reads correctly to anyone who has seen the sheet once. The **icicle thumbnail** shows the Partition's silhouette, about 120 px wide, with the ancestor path filled in rubric and labels on hover. Because the silhouette never changes within a Partition, the reader learns where the rubric sits as the reading moves on. That is the stable-position effect that made Explorer fastest on return trips in the SpaceTree study. The **local brace** shows the parent and its whole brace of members with the current one inked. Burton's next and previous divisions are visible at once, and Fish's "barrel" after barrel appears on the page. It needs more room than the other two, and it suits the head of the chapter better than a corner mark. The **sign and number** form puts the continuation sign (♈, A, B) and the division numbers in a small cartouche. It is the least visual, and the owner asked for "a visual, not the words".

The brace path is the stronger default, because it is legible without the sheet and takes the least room. The icicle is the better memory aid once the reader knows the sheet. Both should be built from the same node ids that link each unit page to its synopsis node today, so the mark, the outline and the sheet stay one structure.

## Conclusion

The brace table was already an interface in print. Printers built it from stacked metal pieces, at least one reader drew missing braces in by hand, and Burton keyed its leaves to his divisions so that it worked as a map and an index at once. Re-setting it as live type with working links continues that use, which is why the re-set sheet should come before any chart drawn in its place. What remains uncertain is bibliographical and critical, and the technique does not depend on it. The scholarship gathered here (Renaker, Höltgen, Fox, Lund, and an essay on "Diagram and Discourse" seen only as a snippet) was mostly unreadable in this round. No source fixed whether the 1621–1651 synopses were fold-outs, how they differ between editions, or whether anyone has read the engraving's compartments as a key to the synopses. Settling those three points would show whether the front page's compartments are the edition's design or Burton's.
