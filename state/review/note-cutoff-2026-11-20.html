The translators’ notes: where to cut

Each translation pass could add a note to its translation. Every note was rated by a model for its use to a reader (note-rate-v1): 3 worth a place in the margin, 2 of some use, 1 of little use; and its kind. The reader now shows a note only if it is rated 3, not flagged doubtful, and judged sound by the blind auditor: 1,396 notes (a green rule marks them below). Below: notes drawn at random from each rating, to judge whether 2 should be shown too, or 3 is already too generous. Each card: the passage, the machine translation, then the note.

Beginae

Beguines

The Beguines were lay religious communities of women in the Low Countries. Clenard had charge of a Beguine house as part of his benefice.

1.2.4.3-f26Latincontextrated 3audit: soundshown now

Tu Marcellus eris, &c

You shall be Marcellus, etc.

These are Anchises' words about the young Marcellus, Augustus' nephew and Octavia's son, who died early. Burton has Livia faint, but the usual story is that Octavia fainted.

1.2.4.6-f21Latinallusionrated 3audit: soundshown now

uxorem ducat Danaen

let him marry Danaë

Danaë was the princess shut in a tower whom Jupiter reached as a shower of gold. The point is that a rich man can win any bride. Petronius's poem, which Burton quotes just before this ('Secura naviget aura…'), continues with this line.

1.2.4.6-f58Latincontextrated 3audit: soundshown now

Socia ad pistrinam

Socia, off to the mill

Being sent to the mill (pistrinum) was the standard punishment for slaves in Roman comedy. Socia stands for the comic slave name Sosia. Burton expands the phrase loosely.

3.3.1.2-f27Latinallusionrated 3audit: soundshown now

Totam noctem bene & pudice nemini molestus dormiendo transegit; mane autem quum nullius conscius facinoris sibi esset, & inertiae puderet, audisse se aiebat cum dolore calculi solere eam conflictari. Duo praecepta juris una nocte expressit, neminem laeserat & honeste vixerat, sed an suum cuique reddidisset, quaeri poterat. Mutius opinor & Trebatius hoc negassent

He spent the whole night well and chastely, sleeping and troubling nobody; but in the morning, since he was conscious of no deed done and was ashamed of his sluggishness, he said he had heard that she was often troubled with pain of the stone. In one night he fulfilled two precepts of the law: he had injured no one and had lived honestly; but whether he had rendered to each her own might be questioned. Mutius, I think, and Trebatius would have said no.

The joke turns on the three precepts of Roman law in Justinian: live honestly, harm no one, give each their due. The scholar kept the first two but failed in the third, his conjugal 'due'. Mutius (Q. Mucius Scaevola) and Trebatius were Roman jurists. 'eam' makes the bride the one who suffers from the stone, which is odd but is what the text says.

O hateful to the gods, / tearful Melancholy, daughter of Cocytus, / you, brought forth in the dark caverns of Tartarus, / a Fury, whom Megaera bore in her womb / and nursed at her breasts, and into whose mouth, when you were little, / Alecto poured bitter milk; / all the demons together brought you, abominable one, / forth into the light, for the ruin of mortals. / Jupiter hurls no such bolt of lightning, / no storm of the sea rages so, / no violent whirlwind has such force. / Do I endure the savage bites of Cerberus? / Is the venom of Echidna feeding on my limbs? / Or a tunic stained with the gore of Nessus' blood? / This evil is past weeping for and past all cure.

3.2.4.1-f40Latinallusionrated 2doubtful

misitque ad orci pallida, lethi obnubila, obsita tenebris loca

and sent him to the pale regions of Orcus, the cloud-veiled places of death, overspread with darkness

Orcus is the underworld. The phrasing seems to echo the old Latin verse 'pallida leti, nubila tenebris loca' ('pale places of death, clouded with darkness'), which Cicero quotes in the Tusculan Disputations as a line of early Roman tragedy. 3.4.2.1-f52Latinsourcerated 3doubtful

Sunt qui in Fortunae jam casibus omnia ponunt, / Et mundum credunt nullo rectore moveri, / Natura volvente vices, &c. ———

There are some who now put everything down to the chances of Fortune, / and believe the world is moved with no one to govern it, / as Nature brings round her changes, etc. —

Juvenal's text reads 'sunt in fortunae qui casibus omnia ponant / et nullo credant mundum rectore moveri / natura volvente vices et lucis et anni'. Burton changes the word order, uses indicatives in place of subjunctives, adds 'jam', and stops before 'et lucis et anni'.
