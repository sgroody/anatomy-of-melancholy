Burton's "Si" replaces Horace's "qui" and drops the main verb ("flebit", will weep), so his line is a self-contained warning. This applies to Burton's Latin address to the reader, where any censorious reader will be 'blown into jests'. You, whoever you are, I declare: by all means watch you don’t rashly insult the author of this work, or be a quibbler and mock him. No, you shouldn’t secretly criticize him (to say it in a word) because of other people’s judgements, nor, like some little satirist, foolishly object or invent falsehoods. For if, in fact, Democritus Junior is what he says he is, is at least a relative of the elder Democritus, or if his own genius savours ever so slightly of him, then it’s all over for you: he’ll turn on you as both judge and prosecutor (‘as he’s got a petulant spleen’), he’ll puff you up with jokes, crush you with wisecracks – and, I add, he’ll sacrifice you to the god of laughter.Again, I warn you not to quibble lest, while you defame Democritus Junior with insults, or disparage him disgracefully (he feels no ill-will towards you), you should hear from a wise friend what the Abderitans once heard from Hippocrates, when they believed that their well-deserving fellow citizen and countryman Democritus was insane: ‘Really, it’s you, Democritus, who are wise, and the Abderitans who are fools and madmen.’ You have the brains of the Abderitan plebs.You’ve been warned with these few words; I mean, mischievously idle reader, begone!g
Translation from Latin: from LatinLat.But you, whoever you are, I charge you to beware that you do not rashly slander the author of this work, or mock him like a caviller. Nay, do not even silently speak against him on the strength of other men's censure (shall I put it in a word?), lest like a sneering critic you foolishly condemn anything, or falsely misrepresent it. For if he really is such as Democritus Junior presents himself to be, at least akin to the elder Democritus, or savours ever so little of his genius, it is all over with you: he will in turn play both censor and accuser against you (since he has a petulant spleen), he will blow you away into jests, grind you to pieces with witticisms, and, I add moreover, he will sacrifice you to the god of Laughter. Again I warn you, cavil at nothing, lest while you defame Democritus Junior with abuse, or disgracefully revile him, who thinks no ill of you, you should hear from some judicious friend the same that the people of Abdera once heard from Hippocrates, when they took their well-deserving fellow-citizen and countryman Democritus for a madman: Truly you, Democritus, are wise, but the Abderites are fools and mad. You have the wits of the Abdera mob. This much, in brief, I would have you warned of, reader ill at leisure: be gone. Burton cites: Martial, Epigrams (unverified)Burton's Latin address 'to the reader who ill employs his leisure' (male feriatus, 'keeping holiday badly'). 'Nasutulus' (literally 'little sharp-nosed one') means a sneering critic. 'Sales' are both salt and witticisms, which plays on 'grind you to pieces'. 'Petulanti splene' echoes Persius, Satires 1.12 ('sum petulanti splene cachinno'). The 'god of Laughter' is Risus, a deity whose festival Apuleius describes. The sentence 'Ne tu Democrite sapis…' comes from the pseudo-Hippocratic letters about Democritus, which Burton summarises in the sidenote 'Hippoc. epist. Damageto'. 'Abderitanae pectora plebis habes' is Martial, Epigrams 10.25.4; 'pectora' here means minds or wits.
