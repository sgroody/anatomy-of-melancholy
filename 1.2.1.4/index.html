For ‘aspects’, see above, .about Eclipses, Earthquakes That is, to be born on the fourth day after the new moon, or at a time that coincides with an eclipse or earthquake.note the Lord of the geniture In astrology, the ‘lord of the geniture’ was whichever planet was the dominant influence at the time of a person’s nativity. Burton refers here to his horoscope, a copy of which (drawn up in his own hand) is found in one of his books now held in the Bodleian Library, and reproduced on his monument in the cathedral of Christ Church in Oxford. It shows that at his birth (8 February 1577), Saturn was at the highest point in the sky and in the sign of Capricorn, and that the ‘ascendant’ (the sign rising on the eastern horizon) was Aries, one of the ‘houses’ (the twelve parts of the zodiac with particular significance in human affairs) which was ruled and at that point occupied by Mars. The putatively malign influences of Saturn and Mars were thereby thought to be tempered – and hence were ‘fortunate in their houses’.note behold the Horoscope That is, face the sign of the zodiac containing the ‘ascendant’.source the six non-natural things See 1.2.2.1 below.note geniture In the astrological terminology here, ‘directed according to Art’ means to have its time calculated by reference to conjunctions between the ‘significator’ (the planet that signifies the subject of the events him- or herself) and the positions of the ‘promittors’ (or ‘promissors’, planets which prognosticate particular events) in the zodiac; ‘the Hor: moon’ refers to the position of the moon in the horoscope; ‘Hylech’ is the Latin term for the Persian hyleg, which denotes the ruling planet in a nativity, and thereby the determinant of longevity; ‘terms’ are any of the five divisions of each sign of the zodiac, forming the region of increased influence of the planet with which it is associated; ‘fixed stars’ are stars that, in contrast to planets, appear always to occupy the same position in the heavens; and ‘radical promissors’ are planets that are found in the ‘radix’ (the original geniture of the subject).
