About

Robert Burton’s The Anatomy of Melancholy, first published in 1621 under the name of Democritus Junior, dissects melancholy (its kinds, causes, symptoms and cures) with everything Burton had read. This edition gives his sixth edition (1651) in the text Angus Gowland edited for Penguin Classics (2021), word for word{% if profile == "public" %}, without Gowland’s editorial matter{% endif %}, and sets beside it what helps to read him.

Reading the page

Burton’s text runs down the middle; everything the edition adds stands in the margin beside the words it belongs to. Point at a note to light its words, or at the words to light the note; click either to keep them lit. On a narrow screen a note opens when you tap its marker. Where a paragraph has more notes than its margin holds, the rest wait behind “more notes”.

a b c … Burton’s notes
Lettered as in the Penguin edition, with a red rule beside his words; cites marks a note that only gives references, note one that says more. A translation or a Bible verse the edition adds hangs below on a dotted rule.
° restored notes
Burton’s notes that Gowland’s edition leaves out, in grey, from an older edition (below).
trans.
A translation of a foreign passage, in its language’s colour: Latin, Greek, Middle English (Chaucer, in small capitals) and the modern languages. So a translation is never taken for Burton’s own English. The arrow after it opens the other translations and the passage’s source where it is known. Where Burton translates a passage himself, the margin says Author trans. and his English takes the original’s colour. A long verse passage translated line for line is set facing it.
cites · Bible · §
Burton’s abbreviated citations written out (“Hor. lib. 3 Od. 6” becomes Horace, Odes, book 3, ode 6), his own abbreviation on hover. In his running text the author’s name stays and a raised § stands where the reference was, the reference in the margin. A Bible reference gives the verse in the King James Version, folded after three lines.
{% if profile == "private" %}
gloss · note · about · source · see
Gowland’s endnotes, beside the words they are on, by kind: the sense of an old word, an explanation, who someone is, where a quotation comes from, a pointer to another note. On a wide window they stand in the left margin.
{% endif %}
A name, finely underlined
Opens a window on that author; held still for a moment, it stays, and its own names open windows in turn. A cross-reference opens the passage it points to.

The reading options (Aa at the top of each page) choose which notes stand in the margin, source by source; how the foreign passages are read (in the margin, under the original, or in place of it with the original on hover); which translation comes first; Burton’s abbreviations instead of the written-out citations; glosses for obsolete words; the Latin’s colour, the theme and the type size{% if profile == "private" %}; and Gowland’s bracketed translations and the Penguin page numbers{% endif %}. “None: the text as printed” leaves only Burton’s words.

Where it comes from

Every addition carries the sign of its source; point at the sign for its full name.

{% if profile == "private" %}
g Gowland (2021)
His translations and notes, from the Penguin edition. His translations are offered in each passage’s box without his brackets; the brackets in the text and the Penguin page numbers are off by default.
{% endif %}
dj Dell & Jordan-Smith (1927)
The human translations of their all-English edition, public domain in the USA, preferred where they exist.
dm Democritus Minor’s edition
The restored notes come from the nineteenth-century “new edition, corrected, and enriched by translations of the numerous classical extracts, by Democritus Minor”, as Karl Hagen re-edited it against the 1638 edition for Project Gutenberg (#10800; public domain). A note is restored where that edition has one and Gowland’s has none, placed by rule from the words around its marker. Its editor added English to some notes, so notes in English without a reference are left out as most likely his; some notes may differ between editions.
w Webster (1913)
Senses of obsolete words from Webster’s Revised Unabridged Dictionary (public domain), offered only for words whose every sense Webster marks obsolete or archaic; a model ({{ gloss_model }}) chose, sentence by sentence, whether Burton uses one, and which. Where he uses a sense Webster lacks, the gloss is the model’s, with its ◇. Common archaisms such as hath are not glossed.{% if profile == "private" %} Gowland’s own glosses come with his notes.{% endif %}
kjv King James Bible
Project Gutenberg #10 and #124 (its Apocrypha). Burton sometimes numbers the Psalms as the Vulgate does, so a Psalm verse may be the one next to the one he meant.
a model
A translation, a citation the rules could not write out, an identification or a gloss made by a language model. Point at the sign for the model, the prompt and the date; it is unreviewed unless it says otherwise.

Authors’ dates and identifiers come from Wikidata (CC0), only where exactly one candidate matched by rule. Of a quotation’s source, “Burton cites” repeats Burton’s own attribution, which is not proof; “probably” is a stated guess with its basis; “source” is used only for a verified match; an unidentified quotation says so.

Finding your way

Search looks through Burton’s text, his notes and, if asked, the translations; its index is loaded the first time you search and kept for offline use. Burton’s synopses stand folded on the front page and at the head of each Partition; rest on a brace to open it. Each Partition’s synopsis also has a page of its own, with the printed sheets. Authors cited lists everyone Burton cites, and The Table, his alphabetical index of 1624, follows the text{% if profile == "private" %}, its page numbers the Penguin edition’s, each linked to the place where that page begins{% else %}; this build leaves out its page numbers, which are the Penguin edition’s{% endif %}. On the front page and in The Argument of the Frontispiece, each panel of the engraved title page leads to its stanza; the panels’ outlines were drawn by machine.
