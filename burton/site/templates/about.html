About this edition

A reading edition of Robert Burton’s The Anatomy of Melancholy, generated from a structured base text with separate layers for translations and notes. This build ({{ profile }} profile, {{ built }}) contains {{ n_units }} text pages.

The text

The base text is Burton’s sixth edition (1651) as edited and modernised by Angus Gowland (Penguin Classics, 2021), taken from the publisher’s EPUB (sha256 {{ source_hash[:16] }}…). Burton’s words, spelling as Gowland gives it, his sidenotes and his headings are kept exactly; corrections appear only as marked corrections with the source’s reading available. Gowland pruned some of Burton’s sidenotes, so the sidenotes here are those of his edition.

What the labels mean

Burton
The text, sidenotes and headings, as edited by Gowland.
{% if profile == "private" %}
Gowland 2021
Gowland’s bracketed translations, interpolations, translation pages, endnote anchors and Penguin page numbers. Labelled wherever they appear; his bracketed translations in the text are hidden by default. His translations are also offered beside the others in each passage’s box, without his brackets, and can stand in for the original; “Reading options” sets which translation comes first.
{% endif %}
Language colours
Latin is set in oxblood by day and candle-gold by night (Reading options can change it), Greek in blue, Middle English in brown and the modern languages in violet, and each passage’s translation in the margin takes its colour. With “Translations in the line”, the English stands in place of the original in that colour and in italic, so it is never mistaken for Burton’s own English; the original shows on hover. In the interlinear mode a translation is set on a faint tint and introduced by its language. A long verse passage whose translation goes line for line is set facing it.
Burton’s own rendering
Where Burton translates a quotation himself, his English takes the original’s colour and is linked to it, and the margin says “Author trans.” instead of adding a new translation.
Machine translation
Drafted by a language model. Each one names the model, the prompt version and the date, and is unreviewed unless it says otherwise.
Dell & Jordan-Smith (1927)
The human translations of the 1927 all-English edition, public domain in the USA, preferred where available.
Expanded citations
Burton’s abbreviated sidenote citations, read by rule (“Hor. lib. 3 Od. 6” becomes Horace, Odes, book 3, ode 6). The abbreviation and title tables are a machine draft that names its model. Where the rules leave a citation unresolved, a model’s expansion is shown with its ◇ label, and only for the very words it was made for; an unidentified citation is left as Burton wrote it. Burton’s own abbreviations show on hover, or in place of the expansions with “Burton’s abbreviations” in Reading options. A citation in his running text keeps the author’s name, with a raised § where the reference stood; the reference itself is in the margin. See Authors cited.
Bible verses (KJV)
Where Burton cites a chapter and verse, the margin gives the verse in the King James Version (Project Gutenberg #10 and #124, its Apocrypha; public domain), under his reference. Burton sometimes numbers the Psalms as the Vulgate does, so a Psalm verse may be the one next to the one he meant.
Wikidata
Authors’ dates and identifiers, from Wikidata (CC0), taken only where exactly one candidate matched by rule.
Obsolete-word glosses
Off by default (Reading options). Each gloss carries its source’s sign, the name on hover.{% if profile == "private" %} g (Gowland 2021) is Gowland’s own endnote gloss, found by rule where his note glosses a word just before its anchor (private build only).{% endif %} w (Webster 1913) is a sense from Webster’s Revised Unabridged Dictionary of 1913 (public domain), offered only for words whose every sense Webster marks obsolete or archaic; a model ({{ gloss_model }}) chose, in each sentence, whether Burton uses one of those senses, and which. Where Burton uses an obsolete sense Webster lacks, the gloss is “drafted by” the model, which names itself. Common archaisms such as hath and saith are not glossed.
° Restored notes
Gowland’s edition leaves out some of Burton’s sidenotes. Where the nineteenth-century text of Project Gutenberg #10800 (public domain, checked by its editor against the 1638 edition) has a note at a place where Gowland has none, it is restored, placed by rule from the words around its marker, and shown in grey with a ° marker and its source. That editor added English translations to some notes; notes in English without a reference are therefore left out, as most likely his and not Burton’s. Some notes may also differ between editions.
Burton cites / Probably / Source
“Burton cites” repeats Burton’s own attribution, which is not proof. “Probably” is a stated guess with its basis. “Source” is used only for a verified match. Unidentified quotations say so.

Finding things

Search looks through Burton’s text, his sidenotes and, if asked, the translations, each found translation labelled with its source. Its index is loaded the first time you search and then kept for offline use. Burton’s Table, his alphabetical index of 1624, follows the text{% if profile == "private" %}; its page numbers are the Penguin edition’s, each linked to the place where that page begins{% else %}; this build leaves out its page numbers, which are the Penguin edition’s{% endif %}. On the home page and in The Argument of the Frontispiece, each panel of the engraved title page links to the stanza that describes it; the panels’ outlines were drawn by machine from the image and are labelled so.

How machine-produced items are made

Translations and language rulings are produced by a resumable script that calls a model with a fixed, versioned prompt and records the model, prompt version, date and input for every result. Gowland’s translations are never given to the model. The records in this build:

Type and tools

Set in EB Garamond, with Greek in GFS Didot (the Greek Font Society) and Cardo for the apothecary and astrological signs EB Garamond lacks (all SIL Open Font License, subset to the characters used). Chaucer is set in small capitals. Latin and Greek are hyphenated at build time with Claudio Beccari’s Latin patterns (MIT licence) and Pyphen’s Greek patterns. The site works offline once loaded.
